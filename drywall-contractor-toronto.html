Condo units, semis and pre-war houses across Toronto. We work to the building’s rules, book the service elevator, and keep the dust out of the corridor.

Toronto is really two different jobs wearing the same name. A good share of what we do here is inside a condo unit, where the drywall is the easy part and the building is the hard part. The rest is in the older houses east and west of downtown, where the walls are not drywall at all.

In a condo, the work itself is straightforward: patching a ceiling after a leak from the unit above, taking out a wall, building a bulkhead, repainting the unit. What takes planning is everything around it. Property management usually wants a certificate of insurance before you go on the schedule, the service elevator is booked as a window rather than a day, and the corridor outside your door is shared space that has to stay clean.

In a pre-war house in Riverdale, Leslieville, the Annex or the Beaches, the original walls are plaster over wood lath and they sit proud of modern board. Patching one with half-inch drywall and walking away leaves a visible step across the wall. That is a taping and mudding problem more than a hanging problem, and it is the most common thing we are called in to correct on somebody else’s work.

Every quote is free, and we will tell you at the visit whether your job needs a permit.

Condo Work

Working in a Toronto Condo: What the Building Will Ask For

The drywall in a condo is no harder than the drywall in a house. Everything that makes a condo job different happens before a single sheet comes off the truck.

Certificate of insurance
Most property managers want proof of liability insurance naming the corporation before they will put you on the calendar. We carry it and send it ahead, and that is usually what decides whether a job starts this week or next month.
The service elevator
Booked in advance, padded, and yours for a set window rather than the whole day. Everything that needs to come up has to come up inside that window, so the material list is finalised before the booking rather than after it.
Board length
Twelve-foot sheets do not fit in most residential elevators. That means eight-foot board, which means more butt joints, which are the hardest seams to hide. Worth knowing because it changes how the finishing is done, not just how the material arrives.
Permitted work hours
Most condo declarations restrict noisy work to weekday daytime hours. Screwing and sanding both fall inside that. It is the main reason a condo job takes more calendar days than the identical job in a house.
Dust containment
Poly at the entry and containment inside the unit. The corridor is shared, and drywall dust tracked into it is the fastest way to get a complaint filed against your job.
Parking and the loading dock
Confirmed before the first day rather than discovered on it. Downtown, this is often the detail that sets the start date.

None of this is difficult. It is just a list, and a contractor who has not worked through it before is a contractor whose start date is going to move.
