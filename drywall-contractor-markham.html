Markham’s housing is mostly newer than what we work on elsewhere in the GTA, and it asks for a higher finish standard. That sounds like a sales line. It is actually geometry.

A nine or ten foot ceiling over an open-concept great room gives you long, unbroken walls. Fewer doors and corners means seams run further before anything interrupts them, and a seam with nothing to break it up has nowhere to hide. Add the pot lights and tall windows that come with those houses and you are lighting the wall from an angle that reveals every ripple. That combination is why Level 5 finishing comes up far more often in Markham than in older housing.

The other regular call is the opposite of a defect. New houses move as the framing dries out, and the nail pops and hairline corner cracks that follow in the first couple of years are normal rather than a sign that anything was built badly. They are worth fixing once, properly, after the movement has settled down.

Finish Levels

Why Newer Markham Homes Ask for a Higher Finish

Finish level is the single thing most worth understanding before you get quotes, because it is where two prices for the same wall come apart.

Critical lighting
Pot lights washing down a wall, or a tall window throwing light along it, is the harshest test a finish ever gets. Work that would look flawless in a hallway shows every seam under that light. It is the usual reason a homeowner calls us about a wall the builder already signed off on.
Level 4 versus Level 5
Level 4 is the normal residential standard: taped, with three coats over seams and screw heads. Level 5 adds a skim coat across the entire surface, so there is no difference in texture between the seam and the board face. Under critical lighting, Level 5 is the only one that holds up.
Long walls, fewer breaks
Open-concept layouts remove the doors and corners that used to interrupt a wall. Seams get longer, and a long seam is meaningfully harder to hide than a short one.
Settlement in the early years
Nail pops and small corner cracks appear as new framing dries and shrinks. Expected, not a defect. Refastening and refinishing once the movement has stopped is what makes the repair last.
Two-storey foyers and stairwells
Scaffold work rather than ladder work. That is a real difference in time and setup, and a quote that does not mention access is a quote that will change.
Basements with rough-ins already in
Newer houses often come with the plumbing rough-in and floor drain already placed. The framing plan follows where those sit, which is worth knowing before you settle on a layout.

We will tell you where Level 5 earns its cost on your job and where it does not, which on most houses is most of the rooms.

How We Finish for Critical Lighting

  1. Look at the wall litWe check the wall under the light it will actually live with. That is what decides whether Level 5 is worth the money on your job, and often it is not.
  2. Coat, then let it dryMulti-coat taping with real drying time between coats. Compressing this is what causes the cracked seams that turn up a year later.
  3. Sand and check under a lightA work light held flat against the wall shows what a ceiling fixture will show later. Anything it catches gets another pass before we move on.
  4. Skim the whole surfaceWhere Level 5 is called for, a thin coat across the entire wall so that seam and board face share one texture.
  5. Prime for the surfaceA high-build or levelling primer before topcoat, because a skim coat and a bare paper face absorb paint differently and it shows if you ignore it.
