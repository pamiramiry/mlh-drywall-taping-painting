Most of North York went up between the early 1950s and the late 1970s, and that single fact predicts most of the work we do there. Bungalows, backsplits and semis from that period share a specific set of problems, and they are not the problems you get in a new subdivision.

The two calls we take most often are popcorn ceiling removal and basement finishing. Stipple was the standard ceiling finish for that entire build period, so a very large share of North York ceilings still carry it. And those basements were poured as storage rather than as living space, which is why finishing one here involves more than it does in a house built with the basement already in mind.

The third regular call is repair in the oldest stock, where the walls are plaster over wood lath rather than board and a patch has to be built up rather than simply filled.

Post-War Housing

What a Post-War North York House Actually Needs

These come up on nearly every North York job. None of them are unusual, but all of them are easier to plan for than to discover halfway through.

Stipple ceilings and asbestos
Ceiling texture applied before the mid-1980s can contain asbestos, and North York has a great deal of housing from exactly that window. A test costs very little next to the cost of getting it wrong, so we recommend having the ceiling tested before any scraping begins and we would rather push a start date than skip it.
Basement headroom
Joists in a 1950s or 1960s bungalow often sit at seven feet or a little under. Once a ceiling goes in you are into the numbers that decide whether a room counts as habitable, so headroom gets measured at the quote rather than discovered later.
Ducts and bulkheads
Old trunk ducting usually runs the length of the basement, right where you want the ceiling to be. Boxing it into a deliberate bulkhead reads as a design choice. Leaving it to be worked around never does.
Steel posts and beams
Mid-century basements almost always have a steel post or two mid-span. They can be framed and boarded into a column or the end of a half wall, but they cannot be moved without an engineer.
Plaster over lath
The oldest stock is plaster on wood lath, which is thicker than modern board. A patch has to be built up to match, or the repair shows as a ridge under any raking light.
New work meeting old
Willowdale and Bayview see a lot of additions and second storeys. The awkward part is never the new framing, it is the joint where a new wall meets a sixty-year-old one that is not quite plumb.

We would rather raise all of this at the quote than send you a change order later.

How a North York Basement Job Runs

  1. Measure the headroom firstBefore anything else. It decides the ceiling approach and, in some houses, whether the room can legally be a bedroom at all.
  2. Plan around the mechanicalsDucts, the panel, the water shutoff and the cleanout all have to stay reachable. That plan gets drawn before framing rather than worked around after it.
  3. Frame and insulateWalls held off the concrete and insulated to suit a below-grade space.
  4. Board, tape and sandMulti-coat taping with real drying time between coats, then sanded flat and checked under a raking light before paint.
  5. Floor and finishVinyl plank flooring over a checked subfloor, then trim and two coats of paint.
